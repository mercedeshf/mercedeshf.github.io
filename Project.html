Project Overview

Crime and Security in Mexico:
A Decade of Trends (2010-2024)

Data Visualization • Mercedes Hernández Ferrer • LSE 2025

Mexico has a history of insecurity concerns. After the War on Drugs declared by President Felipe Calderon (2006-2012) which caused extreme violence in the country, following administrations campaigned explicitly on pacifying the country. The most recent official data available (2024) show a small decline in crimes (SENSP, 2025), as can be seen in Graph 1. Nonetheless, in 2025, 64% of Mexicans reported insecurity as their main concern, almost doubling health, the second highest worry (34%), according to the national statistics office (INEGI, 2018).

This leads to the central question of this project:

Has Mexico’s security situation improved in ways that meaningfully reduce violence and fear?

To answer the question, the project will focus on high impact crime trends, as well as insecurity perception trends, both nationally and state-level. High impact crimes are defined here as those that are the most violent and produce the most fear, which include the following eight types of crime: kidnapping, extortion, homicide, feminicide, robbery with violence, domestic violence, drug trafficking and sexual crimes.

Conclusions

The data suggests a bifurcated reality in Mexico's security landscape. While administrative efforts and policy changes have successfully reduced common property crimes, the mechanisms driving high-impact violence—specifically homicide and gender-based violence—remain resistant to current interventions.

Furthermore, the analysis of historical cycles indicates that current violence levels are not merely anomalies but part of a structural issue that has evolved since 2008. Future policy must differentiate between the drivers of opportunistic crime (theft) and organized violence.

Methodology & Data Challenges

Data Sources

World Bank API

Indicator VC.IHR.PSRC.P5. Used for international comparisons to standardize homicide rates across different legal systems.

INEGI (National Statistics)

Vital Statistics and Public Security Census (1990-2024). This provided the granular domestic data for all state-level analysis.

Data Challenges

The "Dark Figure" of Crime

Official reports significantly undercount crime due to low reporting rates (approx. 93% of crimes in Mexico go unreported). This analysis relies on reported incidents, which acts as a proxy but not a total census of violence.

Classification Changes

In 2015, the methodology for classifying "Feminicide" changed in several states, creating a statistical artifact where rates appear to spike simply due to reclassification rather than an increase in events.
