Project

Crime and Security in Mexico:

Has Mexico’s security situation improved in ways that meaningfully reduce violence and fear?

Data Visualization • Mercedes Hernández Ferrer • LSE 2025

Aims of the Project

Mexico has a long history of insecurity. After the War on Drugs declared by President Felipe Calderón (2006–2012), which generated extreme levels of violence, subsequent administrations campaigned on promises to pacify the country. However, in 2025, 64% of Mexicans identified insecurity as their main concern, according to INEGI (2025). This leads to the central question of this project:

Has Mexico’s security situation improved in ways that meaningfully reduce violence and fear?

To answer this, the project analyzes trends in high impact crimes and insecurity perception at both national and state levels. High impact crimes are defined here as those most associated with violence and fear: kidnapping, extortion, homicide, feminicide, robbery with violence, domestic violence, drug trafficking, and sexual crimes.

General picture

Graph 1: Crime

Reported crime shows a generally upward trend since 2015, with only a modest decline between 2023 and 2024. This raises the question of which crimes are driving this increase.

Official institutions measure over 40 types of crime, which I grouped into 10 to simplify. While “other crimes” account for a large share, high impact crimes such as feminicide appear visually marginal, highlighting the difference between crime volume and social impact.

Graph 5: Homicides (1990-2024)

Homicide is commonly used to assess security conditions. Historically, this graph shows that administrations following the War on Drugs failed to pacify the country. Although homicides have declined since 2018, levels remain well above those observed before 2012.

High Impact Crime vs. Perception

Map 1: High Impact Crime vs. Insecurity Perception

While national insecurity perception remained around 70% between 2015 and 2024, state-level experiences varied widely (INEGI, 2025). The map reveals sharp divergences as well as states with high perceived insecurity despite lower crime levels, and the opposite.

High Impact Crime vs. Perception

Graph 6: High Impact Crime vs. Insecurity Perception

Tracking trends over time shows that only 5 of 32 states have non-increasing high impact crime rates, and just 6 display a divergence between crime and perception trends from 2015 to 2024. In most states, violence and fear move together rather than independently.

Conclusions

The evidence suggests that Mexico’s security situation has not improved in ways that meaningfully reduce both violence and fear. Although homicide rates have declined slightly in recent years, they remain far above pre-War on Drugs levels. High impact crimes constitute a significant share of total crime and follow trends similar to overall criminal activity, shaping both violence and insecurity perception.

At the state level, most entities show parallel trends in high impact crime and insecurity perception, suggesting that public fear largely reflects lived experiences rather than exaggerated perceptions. Overall, recent improvements have not been sufficient to alter the structural dynamics of violence or widespread insecurity in Mexico.

Data & Challenges

Data Used

Data Challenges

Mexican official data was used from the Instituto Nacional de Estadística y Geografía (INEGI) and the Secretariado Ejecutivo del Sistema Nacional de Seguridad Pública (SESNSP). Homicide data from the World Bank API was used for Graph 5. The information used is crime data from SESNSP; homicide data from World Bank Data; insecurity perception data and population census data from INEGI.

Data Challenges

A key challenge was coherently representing high impact and total crime using SESNSP data, which classifies over 40 crimes with multiple subtypes. Transparency was ensured by first presenting general crime categories (Graph 2) before focusing on high impact crimes, and by documenting all grouping decisions in a Google Colab notebook.

As this project deals with a question relating to reducing violence and fear, I propose an approach dealing with high impact crimes only, as defined above. Therefore, the data was cleaned and crimes were aggregated into the high impact categories proposed.

A second challenge is that INEGI population census data is updated every five years, with the most recent data from 2020. As a result, crime rates per 100,000 inhabitants for 2015-2024 are normalized using the 2020 census.

All data cleaning work can be accessed through Google Colab notebooks referenced below. All data (csvs, jsons, xlsx) used in this project can be found in my Github repository, where files are referenced with the same name for accessibility and transparency.

When comparing insecurity perception with high impact crimes, crimes were not weighted by severity, despite their unequal influence on fear (e.g., homicide versus violent robbery), to avoid methodological disputes over relative importance.

Note on automation: To avoid system instability, Mexican data APIs were not used directly. Instead, a World Bank API was used, with homicide data that matches INEGI figures.

Finally, all crime data suffers from underreporting: approximately, 90% of crimes are not reported in Mexico (INEGI, 2025). This project does not resolve this limitation and relies solely on officially reported data.
